The PerilOrder method

Optimal resilience investment. PerilOrder identifies the best flood resilience investment for a community. Flood costs across every severity are integrated into one expected annual figure, benefit-cost analysis is aligned to FEMA standards, and the output includes an annual avoided loss figure a lender can finance against.

Three alternatives, one plant. Johnson's wastewater treatment plant has three published engineering alternatives. The loss counted is both physical damage to the plant and the value of the wastewater service its customers lose while it is out.

What we do. Price mitigation options by avoided loss per dollar, showing which can carry a federal mitigation application.

Our source data and methods

  • Public and client data (public only in this case study)
  • FEMA Hazus depth-damage functions3
  • FEMA's published economic value for interrupted wastewater service4
  • Capital costs reported for three Vermont plant projects2.1

What comes from our clients. Output data for the infrastructure under analysis. In Johnson's case, four figures: the plant's 250,000 gallon per day rating, roughly $3M of emergency repairs and about 30 days out of service after July 2023, and the published cost of its reconstruction proposal.1

What we model

  • The damage fraction and outage days at each flood severity
  • The population served
  • How effective each option is
  • The capital costs of two of the three options, which are benchmarks from Hardwick and Montpelier rather than estimates for Johnson

Who this is for. Local planners who need to prioritize resilience investment, supporting engineering studies, appraisals and benefit-cost analysis prepared to FEMA's submission standard.

Why not protect roads or other infrastructure?

FEMA requirements. FEMA counts avoided loss of service for utilities at a published value per person per day, and for roads it counts avoided delay, which needs traffic volume rural Vermont does not have.

Rural roads rarely clear. A road clears the federal threshold on avoided delay only at volumes around 8,000 vehicles a day.2.4 Vermont corridors of the kind damaged in 2023 and 2024 carry a fraction of that, so road projects fail benefit-cost even when the repair bill is large and recurring.

A plant is the opposite case. Johnson's plant was out of service about 30 days after July 2023,1 and every day it is down carries a countable cost for every household it serves.

The limit of this analysis. Strip the service benefit out and all three options here fail, the best reaching 0.69x. The case rests on FEMA's service value and on the outage curve.

What this means for Johnson
  • Physical damage alone cannot carry a federal application. At $250,750 a year, the best any option reaches on avoided repair cost by itself is 0.69x.
  • Two thirds of the annual loss sits above the 1 in 50 flood, so mitigation should be sized to the 2023 crest or higher. Both the levee and the elevation proposal already specify three feet above the 2023 level. Protection designed to the 1 in 25 flood would capture about an eighth of the loss.
  • July 2023 confirms the severe end of the curve. USGS puts the July 2023 peak at its streamgage on the Lamoille River at Johnson between a 1 in 100 and a 1 in 500 annual chance.7 The model assumes 30 days out of service at the 1 in 100 flood, and Johnson was out about 30 days.1

The three assumptions that move the result. Outage days drive 46 percent of expected annual loss and are anchored on one observed event. The 80 gallon per capita ratio sets population served and scales service loss one for one. The damage fractions and the replacement value set the remaining 54 percent. A follow-on engagement replaces all three with the utility's own flow records, customer count and a site elevation survey.
Where this is weakest. Outage days are anchored on a single observed event at this plant. If July 2023 sat nearer the 1 in 500 end of the USGS range than the 1 in 100 end, the outage curve used here overstates the tail and the true expected annual loss is lower than $467,312.
Limitations carried by this section. Replacement value is taken from a press-reported reconstruction estimate rather than an appraisal. Damage fractions and outage days by flood severity and population served are labelled assumptions, not measurements. FEMA's $66 per person-day wastewater service value was located through secondary research rather than by retrieving the FEMA report, so it should be confirmed before it is quoted in a filing. The USGS exceedance probabilities cited for July 2023 are marked preliminary in the source report.

Johnson needs one funding decision this year, and two later

File the lift station package now, survey the site before deciding the levee, and move full elevation into the capital plan. Waiting costs $467,312 a year

Recommended sequence

It clears the federal threshold on its own numbers and can be filed against either Vermont declaration.1 It also likely carries itself without a grant, covering $136,413 of annual debt service out of $189,206 of avoided loss, a coverage ratio of 1.39x.4
2
Commission a site elevation survey before deciding the perimeter levee$3.5M · 1.33x
The levee clears here, but the state found Montpelier's equivalent project not viable, and the severities at which this plant takes water are estimated from regional flood frequency rather than surveyed elevations. The survey decides this option and costs a fraction of the project. If the levee fails it, the Clean Water State Revolving Fund carries no benefit-cost test.2
3
Carry full plant elevation in the capital plan, timed to replacement$17.0M · 0.36x
Spending an asset's full replacement value to protect it cannot clear a benefit-cost ratio of 1.0. The 95 percent protection arrives regardless in a rebuild sited above the flood elevation. Johnson's decision is when to replace the plant, rather than whether to elevate it.
What this means for Johnson
  • Only the first item needs a decision this year. The survey is a small procurement and elevation is a capital-plan entry, so the near-term choice is a single grant filing.
  • Both clearing options likely carry their own debt service. At 5 percent over 20 years the lift station package covers at 1.39x and the levee at 1.20x out of avoided loss.4 A lender would underwrite rate revenue and the covenant rather than the avoided loss, so these figures indicate affordability rather than credit approval.

Method and assumptions

On the cost shares. The federal share is stated as typically 75 percent and flagged rather than asserted, because cost share varies by program, by declaration and by applicant type. Nothing in the benefit-cost results depends on it. It affects only how much of the $1.7M the utility carries.

What would change the answer
  • A site elevation survey. The severities at which the plant takes water come from regional flood frequency and mapped flood zone, not from surveyed finished floor elevations. This decides the levee.
  • The utility's own outage records. The outage curve is built from the one documented event at this plant, roughly 30 days after July 2023. Johnson's operating logs would replace it, and that curve drives 46 percent of the annual loss.
  • A customer count. Population served is derived from rated flow at 80 gallons per capita per day. A real count scales service benefit one for one, and with it every ratio here.
  • Replacement value. Set at $17.0M from Johnson's own reconstruction estimate. Capacity-exponent scaling of Montpelier's plant suggests $18.7M to $23.0M, so a higher value would raise every ratio.
  • An engineer's estimate for the lift station scope. The $1.7M is a Hardwick plant project used as a benchmark.
