The PerilOrder method

Optimal allocation of a fixed mitigation budget. PerilOrder models expected loss across every segment of an insured book, measures what a given retrofit removes from that loss, and allocates a fixed budget to maximise loss avoided per dollar. The outputs are a ranked allocation, the loss avoided per year, and the payback period on the spend.

Twenty-six segments, one budget. PerilOrder analyzes NCIUA's residential book at county-and-zone level, 26 segments in all, because beach and coastal areas are rated differently and funded by different grant programs.

What we do. Rank mitigation spend by avoided loss per dollar, and show what the current distribution costs in loss not avoided.

Our source data and methods

  • Public and client data (public only in this case study)
  • Filed rating-territory hurricane loss costs from the North Carolina Rate Bureau1.2
  • Exposure and risk counts from NCIUA's statutory financial statements1.1
  • Measured retrofit performance from NCIUA's own claims study2.2

What comes from our clients. Exposure, policy and claims data for the book under analysis. In NCIUA's case, four public figures stand in: residential liability of $151,206,985,500 across 256,317 risks, roofs completed and approved by county, the $30,818,006 grant budget, and the 22.7 percent loss reduction NCIUA measured.1.1, 2.2

What we model

  • Expected hurricane loss per property in each segment, scaled from filed territory loss costs to segment average insured value
  • Avoided loss per grant dollar, at the grant cap for each program
  • Annual take-up capacity, capped at 10 percent of untreated stock
  • The allocation itself, which is derived here and is not an NCIUA figure

Who this is for. Residual market insurers and the departments that oversee them, who already spend the money and already hold the claims risk, supporting rate filings, mitigation program design and reinsurance submissions.

What this analysis excludes, and why

Storm surge is out of scope. Homeowners policies exclude flood and a FORTIFIED roof does not address surge, so no surge loss appears in these loss costs. Surge is a large share of hurricane damage on this coast and nothing here speaks to it.

Non-hurricane wind and hail is out of scope. The same filing prices it between $318 and $426 across territories, far flatter than hurricane, so including it would compress the gaps without reordering them.1.2

The NCJUA FAIR Plan book is out of scope. This covers the Coastal Property Insurance Pool only.

Every loss figure here is ground-up, not net. Loss costs assume a $1,000 flat deductible while NCIUA applies percentage named storm deductibles, and much of the book is wind-only rather than full homeowners. The $540M of modelled annual loss is therefore loss on the insured properties, not loss NCIUA retains, and it is materially higher than the net figure.1.6 The effect applies across every segment and is close to neutral to the ranking.

This ranks average annual loss, not the tail. NCIUA's reinsurance and its Cape Lookout Re notes attach high in the loss distribution. An allocation efficient against average annual loss is not automatically efficient against the layers it transfers, which is where section 4 stops.

Method and assumptions
Exposure, and the beach and coastal split

County exposure comes from the Aggregate Liability by Counties exhibit of NCIUA's statutory financial statements at 31 December 2025.1 Residential liability totals $151,206,985,500 across 256,317 risks, which the figures here reconcile to exactly. The exhibit reports seven residential liability columns, four beach and three coastal.

Beach = Dwelling Beach + Homeowner Beach + HO Wind Beach + Dwelling Beach Wind Coastal = Homeowner Coastal + HO Wind Coastal + Dwelling Coastal Wind

Average insured value is segment liability divided by segment count. The exhibit excludes dwelling other coverages, so these understate total amounts at risk.

Rating territory

Territories follow the NCDOI settlement of the 2024 homeowners filing.2 Beach areas of Currituck, Dare and Hyde are 110. Beach areas of Brunswick, Carteret, New Hanover, Onslow and Pender are 120. Coastal areas of Currituck, Dare, Hyde and Pamlico are 130. Eastern coastal areas of the southern five counties are 140, western coastal areas are 160, and the nine northeastern coastal counties are 150. NCDOI separates 140 from 160 by ZIP code, which is not public, so southern coastal exposure takes a 50/50 blend at $581.30.

Hazard

Modelled annual hurricane loss costs by territory come from page C-7 of the Rate Bureau's 2024 homeowners filing.2 Aon produced them by blending AIR and RMS hurricane output on trended latest-year exposures, adding 6 percent for loss adjustment expense, and dividing by earned house-years and the trended average rating factor. Territory 110 is $1,177.25, 120 is $1,446.48, 130 is $490.94, 140 is $812.73, 150 is $299.99 and 160 is $349.87. These are filed actuarial indications; the settlement approved premiums, not each component.

Expected and avoided loss
loss per risk = territory loss cost × (average insured value / 200,000) avoided per $1,000 = (loss per risk / grant cost) × 1,000

Summed across all 26 segments the model gives $540M of modelled ground-up hurricane annual loss on the insured properties, before any mitigation. Loss addressed is the modelled loss sitting on the homes a plan funds. Loss avoided is that figure multiplied by 0.227.2.2 The same factor applies to every segment, so it scales all allocation rules equally and does not move the ranking.

Calibration: what the $540M is, and is not

It is ground-up loss on the insured properties at the filing's $1,000 flat deductible. It is not NCIUA's expected retained loss, and two checks show the gap.

  • Against earned premium. $540M is 68.5 percent of the $788,606,415 earned in 2025,1 before non-hurricane wind, expenses and the $194M reinsurance spend. No insurer operates at that loss ratio.
  • Against a known event. Hurricane Florence cost NCIUA $1,478,009,913.6 An annual expected loss of $540M is 37 percent of that, which on a pure frequency reading implies a Florence-scale loss roughly every 2.7 years.

The gap is the deductible and the policy form. NCIUA applies percentage named storm deductibles rather than $1,000, and much of the book is wind-only. Converting ground-up loss to retained loss needs either a deductible relativity table from the filing or NCIUA's own policy data, and neither has been applied here.

Why the ranking survives it. A percentage deductible scales with Coverage A, so it removes approximately the same fraction of gross loss at every insured value, which makes the adjustment close to a uniform multiplier across segments. If deductible percentages or policy forms vary systematically by territory, that no longer holds, and only policy-level data settles it.

Treating all southern coastal exposure as territory 160 gives an index of 151, a 50/50 blend gives 143, and all 140 gives 136. The split does not affect the top seven segments, which are all beach.

  • Dare beach looks like the obvious place to spend, and property for property it is not. Method and assumptions
How loss avoided and payback are calculated

Modelled hurricane loss per property is the filed territory loss cost scaled to each segment's average insured value. Loss addressed is that figure summed across the homes a plan funds. Loss avoided is loss addressed multiplied by 0.227, NCIUA's own finding that a FORTIFIED roof cuts loss per building by 22.7 percent in a named storm.2

Payback is the one-time spend divided by annual avoided loss, with no discounting applied. No roof life is assumed. A roof does not last forever and a FORTIFIED designation is time limited, so the benefit runs for the life of the roof rather than indefinitely. That is why the result is expressed as payback years rather than a present value.

Grant cost and the three allocation rules

Strengthen Your Roof pays up to $10,000 in rating territories 110 and 120, and Strengthen Your Coastal Roof pays up to $6,000 in territories 130, 140, 150 and 160.3 Both are modelled at cap, and awards below cap would buy more roofs per dollar. Those territory sets correspond exactly to the beach and coastal split used throughout.

  • Current allocation distributes the budget in proportion to roofs already funded per segment
  • PerilOrder funds segments in descending order of return until the budget runs out, capped by take-up capacity
  • Pro rata to risk count distributes by headcount and avoids $2.01M, worse than today, because it ignores where the hazard concentrates
Sensitivity to the effectiveness figure

Avoided loss is loss addressed multiplied by the effectiveness factor, so that factor cancels from any comparison between allocations. The gain is the same at every plausible value, and only the dollar amounts and payback years move.

What the measured figure does not control for. The study compares NCIUA claims at geocoded RenaissanceRe wind speeds,2 so it holds modelled hazard intensity roughly constant, which is the largest confounder. It does not control for building age, construction quality or owner self-selection, and grant recipients applied while post-loss endorsement roofs went to homes that had already claimed. Both groups may differ systematically from the comparison group, so 22.7 percent should be read as an observed difference rather than a causal effect.6

Uniform mitigation effect

The 22.7 percent loss reduction is applied identically to every segment. NCIUA's own filed rate credits vary by territory,5 and re-running the allocation with each of the five filed credit tables leaves the top seven segments in the same order and funds the same two segments on four of the five, raising the gain to between 54 and 56 percent. The homeowner table is the exception: it puts Brunswick beach ahead of Currituck and the gain becomes 46 percent, with the money still going to the same two beaches. The uniform assumption is the conservative choice.

What the segment ranking does not capture
  • Segment averages stand in for properties, which is the largest limitation on this page and the reason it understates what property-level ranking would find
  • Eligible capacity is overstated, since every risk is treated as a candidate while roof age, condition and prior FORTIFIED status shrink the real pool
  • Policy mix is not modelled; loss costs are homeowners base class while much of the book is wind only
  • Timing differs: exposure at 31 December 2025, grants at 30 September 2025, loss costs from a January 2024 filing settled January 2025

What this means for NCIUA
  • 3,082 roofs across five beach segments, against 3,616 roofs spread across all 26 today. Fewer roofs, more loss avoided, because the roofs sit where the hazard and the insured values concentrate.
  • No coastal segment is funded, and that is a policy question rather than a modelling one. The rule applied here is to maximise modelled hurricane loss avoided per grant dollar and nothing else. Settling whether NCIUA is budget constrained or demand constrained decides how much of this gain is real.
Method and assumptions
The cap, and what it does to the answer

The 10 percent cap is a planning assumption, not an NCIUA figure.1 Untreated stock uses roofs completed and approved by segment from the October 2025 association update.2 Without a cap the model lets a segment absorb grants up to its entire untreated stock. Untreated stock is insured risks less roofs already funded or approved.

  • No cap: $3.71M avoided, a 43 percent gain, two segments funded
  • 20 percent cap: $3.37M, a 30 percent gain, four segments
  • 10 percent cap: $3.08M, a 19 percent gain, five segments
  • 5 percent cap: $2.74M, a 6 percent gain, spread wide

Historical delivery sits near 2 to 3 percent of segment stock per year, but the program appears to have been limited by budget rather than by demand for most of that period, so the realistic ceiling is unknown. At a 5 percent cap the advantage of ranking largely disappears.

What this means for NCIUA
  • The prize is bigger than the claims saving, and the size is unconfirmed. NCIUA ceded $194,090,579 of reinsurance premium in 2025.1 Reinsurance and catastrophe bond pricing is driven by expected loss to each layer, which comes from the exceedance probability curve behind the book.
  • The book is 6 percent FORTIFIED today,2 which is why where the next roof goes still matters. For scale, Hurricane Florence cost NCIUA $1,478,009,913 across 71,917 claims.2
  • The Cape Lookout Re resilience spread is a second pot needing allocation. It returns roughly $2M per risk period to NCIUA earmarked for FORTIFIED roofs if aggregate losses stay below 60 percent of attachment.3 Because it is tied to a trigger rather than to average loss, ranking it properly needs the same curve.
Method and assumptions

How the saving is derived. Avoided loss is taken as a share of the $540M ground-up modelled book loss, 0.48 percent as currently allocated and 0.69 percent under the ceiling, and that share is applied to ceded premium.4 Numerator and denominator use the same ground-up basis, so the percentage is unaffected by the gross-to-net gap set out in section 1. It is derived here and is not an NCIUA figure.

Three reasons this is provisional
  • It assumes ceded premium moves one for one with modelled loss. There is no basis for that beyond the mechanism being directionally right
  • It measures average annual loss, not the tail. Reinsurance and the Cape Lookout Re notes attach high in the loss distribution, and an allocation efficient against average annual loss is not automatically efficient against the layers NCIUA transfers
  • Confirming it needs the exceedance probability curve behind the risk transfer program, which is not public. That is the single most useful thing NCIUA could hand over

This section sizes the prize and names the data that would settle it. It is not a quotable figure.
